Charlotte-Mecklenburg · Human-Centered AI class project

Homelessness should be rare, brief, and non-recurring.

That's Charlotte-Mecklenburg's own goal. This site uses public data to show what is pushing neighbors out of housing, then maps where AI — including large language models — can help the people and caseworkers already doing this work. The rule we followed: AI supports human judgment and dignity; it never replaces them.

01 · The data

What the numbers say

Charlotte-Mecklenburg measures homelessness two ways. The Point-in-Time (PIT) Count is a one-night January snapshot required by HUD. The One Number is a running count of everyone actively experiencing homelessness in the county's data system. They answer different questions, so we show them separately.

02 · Neighborhood map

Where housing pressure is concentrated

Homelessness counts are county-wide, but the pressures that cause it are not spread evenly. This map uses open data from the Charlotte-Mecklenburg Quality of Life Explorer for 459 Neighborhood Profile Areas (NPAs). Pick a measure, then hover over or tap a neighborhood.

Using this map responsibly

Maps of need can stigmatize neighborhoods. This one is for deciding where to send help, such as outreach, rental assistance, and preserving affordable housing. It is not for labeling places or people. All data is neighborhood totals; no individual is identified.

    05 · Human-centered AI

    Our design rules

    1 · Nothing about us without us

    People with lived experience of homelessness are paid co-designers and reviewers, not test subjects. They can stop a tool.

    2 · A human is always one tap away

    Every AI tool has a clear hand-off to a real person. Danger, children at risk, or legal decisions always go to humans.

    3 · Never invent facts

    LLMs can “hallucinate.” A wrong phone number or address can cost someone a night outside, so tools answer only from verified, dated resource lists.

    4 · Protect privacy and dignity

    Collect the minimum. No individual “risk scores.” Data never leaves approved systems. Use person-first language: “people experiencing homelessness.”

    5 · Design for real conditions

    Works on a basic phone by text, on slow data, with a cracked screen and a dying battery. Plain language at a 6th-grade level, English and Spanish.

    6 · Check for unequal outcomes

    Black residents are 74% of people experiencing homelessness here. Every tool is tested for whether it serves everyone equally — and paused if it doesn't.

    7 · Use AI only where it helps

    Some problems need a shared spreadsheet, more shelter beds, or more affordable homes — not a chatbot. AI can't build housing.

    8 · Measure outcomes for people

    Success is people housed faster and fewer people entering homelessness — not chatbot usage or “engagement.”

    If we used an LLM: the guardrail prompt

    A system prompt is the instruction an LLM follows. Ours puts human-centered rules into the model's instructions — but prompts alone aren't enough, so the app also checks every reply against the verified list before showing it.

    You are a resource navigator for people facing
homelessness in Mecklenburg County, NC.

RULES
1. Only share resources from VERIFIED_RESOURCES.
   Never invent a phone number, address, or hour.
   If nothing fits, say so and offer NC 211.
2. If the person mentions danger, self-harm, abuse,
   or a child at risk: give 911 / 988 first and
   flag the chat for a human right away.
3. Write at a 6th-grade reading level. Short
   sentences. Reply in the person's language.
4. Never give legal advice or predict a court
   outcome. Connect to Legal Aid instead.
5. Ask for the minimum: no names, SSNs, or
   immigration status.
6. Be warm and respectful. Use "people
   experiencing homelessness," never "the
   homeless." Never judge.
7. End every reply with: "Want to talk to a
   person? Reply HUMAN."

    07 · Sources & method

    Where this data comes from

    The main public data hub is the Charlotte-Mecklenburg Housing & Homelessness Dashboard, run by Mecklenburg County Community Support Services with the UNC Charlotte Urban Institute. Its annual State of Housing Instability & Homelessness (SoHIH) report, Point-in-Time Count pages, One Number data, and Ecosystem directory are the backbone of this project. Neighborhood data comes from the Quality of Life Explorer (UNC Charlotte Urban Institute, City of Charlotte, and Mecklenburg County), using its open-data repository. Figures were checked on November 10, 2026.
